<p class="lead">Risograph looks effortless in the final print and is anything but during the run. Four hundred sheets in, my hands were stained teal and I had learned the same three lessons about six times each.</p>

The plan was modest: a two-color edition of 200, which — allowing for setup, misfeeds, and registration tests — meant pulling closer to 400 sheets through the drum. I thought I'd be done in an afternoon. I was done in a day and a half, and the difference was entirely things I should have known going in.

## <span class="idx">01</span>Registration is a discipline, not a setting

Two-color riso means running every sheet through twice, once per drum. If the paper shifts even a millimeter between passes, the colors drift apart and the whole sheet reads as a mistake. The fix isn't a clever setting — it's boring consistency: same corner, same feel, every single time.

> The machine is repeatable. I was the variable.

## <span class="idx">02</span>Ink needs time you didn't budget for

Riso ink is soy-based and dries slowly. Stack sheets too soon and the second color offsets onto the back of the sheet above it. I lost about thirty prints learning that a full drying rack is not optional and that "touch dry" and "actually dry" are an hour apart.

## <span class="idx">04</span>Would I do it again

Immediately. There's a texture to a hand-pulled riso print — the slight misregistration, the ink sitting on top of the paper rather than soaking in — that no laser printer will ever fake. The waste sheets went into the sketchbook pile. Nothing here is precious; it's all practice for the next run.
